Why is our profit lower than the report says?

Because the report was missing about £80,000 a year in real costs.

Result
About £80,000 a year found
My role
Bookkeeper
When
April 2026, across four months of weeks

The situation

The owners ran the business off a weekly profit report. Every week it said how much the business had made, and decisions were made on that number: what to spend, what to price, whether a week had been good.

The report worked out its costs from a set of settings built into it. The real costs lived in the accounts, in Xero. Nobody had checked that the two agreed, because the report looked sensible week after week.

What went wrong

Put side by side with Xero, the report's costs came up short by about £1,540 a week. Its settings didn't account for costs the business was really paying, so every week the report said the business had made more than it had.

Over a year that's roughly £80,000 of profit that didn't exist, on the number the owners were using to run the business.

What I did

  1. Kept the books. I was hired to do the bookkeeping, so the real costs were already passing through my hands every week in Xero.
  2. Checked the report against the accounts, line by line. Not the totals, the costs themselves: what the report assumed against what Xero recorded.
  3. Did it for four months of weeks. One bad week proves nothing. Months of them show whether the problem is built in, and something built in can be fixed at the cause.
  4. Traced it to the report's settings. The gap wasn't a mistake in any one week. It was built into how the report worked out its costs.
  5. Put it right at the cause, so the weekly number tells the truth from then on.

How it works for the technical reader

The weekly profit report calculated its costs from configured assumptions rather than from the ledger. I reconciled its cost lines against Xero week by week across about four months, mapping each report category to the accounts behind it, so every difference had a named cause rather than a lump-sum adjustment. The shortfall averaged around £1,540 a week, which annualises to roughly £80,000. Because the cause was in the configuration and not in any one week's entries, the fix went into the configuration, and every week reported afterwards reflects it.

This was bookkeeping, not software. It's the kind of finding that only comes from someone who is already inside the accounts every week, which is why I start any systems work with the numbers.
