Systems that run themselves · Events caterer

We only find out something broke when someone happens to look

Now the system watches itself, and tells the right person what to do.

Result
Caught in a test, not by a customer
My role
Systems & Automation Manager
When
July 2026, started the day after launch

The situation

Once the new booking system went live, the work happened on its own. Quotes, invoices, payments, calendar dates and emails flowed between six tools without anyone re-typing them.

That's the point of it, and also the risk. When a person drops a task, someone usually notices. When an automation drops one, nothing happens at all: the email just never arrives, the payment just never gets marked. Nobody sees a thing until a customer rings.

What went wrong

The day after launch, a booking showed £1,584 when the real total was £1,440. A leftover automation from the old system was still running. It was working out its own price from out-of-date details and writing it over the right one.

It was a test booking, so no customer saw it. But it was only caught because I happened to be looking at that card. The real question was what else was going wrong where nobody was looking.

What I did

  1. Fixed the cause that morning. Switched off every automation left over from the old system, so the new one was the only thing writing prices.
  2. Planned the watching the next day. Listed everything that could fail without anyone noticing, and decided how loud each one should be. Anything touching money or a customer is serious: it emails me and stays open until a person closes it. Background hiccups that fix themselves clear on their own, unless they keep happening.
  3. Built the Observatory. A monitoring screen, admins only:
    • It watches the machine, not the business. No revenue, no lead counts. Just: are the connections up, are the emails landing, did every action produce its result?
    • Six checks that should always read zero, like "paid, but no trailer booked" and "confirmed, but not on the calendar".
    • Every alert says what to do, in plain English. Not an error code: "The customer did not receive this email. Get a correct address or reach them another way, then resend."
    • A test button sends a pretend serious alert, to prove the email actually arrives.
  4. Watched the watcher. A website has no clock, so a scheduled job wakes the system up to run its checks. Scheduled jobs can die too, so an outside service, Healthchecks.io, emails me if the job stops checking in.

How it works for the technical reader

Alerts live in their own MySQL table, one row per distinct failure, keyed by its source and a stable error signature so a repeating timeout counts up instead of piling up. Self-healing background jobs raise low-severity alerts that close themselves on the next clean run; money and customer paths raise high-severity ones that never auto-close. A low-severity alert that fails three times in a row is promoted and emails me once. A failed-for-good action becomes an open high-severity row with enough context to redo it by hand, so the alert table doubles as the dead-letter queue. Error text is scrubbed of connection details before it is stored or emailed.

The screen also shows a heartbeat, a merged activity feed, a log of every settings change, and whether the live code matches the repository. A scheduled job runs the checks, and Healthchecks.io expects it to check in every hour, with 30 minutes' grace, before it emails.
