Systems that run themselves · Events caterer

Can we switch systems without stopping the business?

One evening, about 130 checks first, and a way back at every step.

Result
About 130 checks. No downtime.
My role
Systems & Automation Manager
When
4 July 2026, about 90 minutes

The situation

The new system was finished. The business was still running on the old one. Every enquiry, quote, invoice and payment was flowing through the old setup, and customers were mid-booking on it.

This is the part most businesses put off, because it's where things go wrong. Stop the system for an evening and you lose enquiries. Switch carelessly and you lose a booking someone has already paid for.

What nearly went wrong

A rule in the new system closes any quote older than 14 days. Sensible on its own: old quotes shouldn't sit open forever.

But at the moment of the switch there were real bookings carrying quotes older than that, customers who were still deciding. Switching the rule on with everything else would have quietly closed live bookings on day one. I found it while working through the checks, and held that one rule back until the old quotes had been dealt with by hand.

What I did

  1. Proved it worked before it touched anyone. About 130 checks across the whole journey, run against the live system in a rehearsal: a real invoice issued and then voided, the calendar and the money paths exercised, and nothing left behind afterwards.
  2. Ran the new system alongside the old one, switched off. Every action it could take was rehearsed in a mode that changed nothing: no real invoice, no real email. That's how the team practised too.
  3. Went through the live bookings one by one. 30 of them: 23 carried across, 7 closed as dead. Each one checked, not batch-imported and hoped for.
  4. Kept in-flight customer links alive. Anyone holding a quote or form link from the old system still had it work after the switch, because the old addresses were bridged to the new ones.
  5. Wrote the switch as a runbook. An ordered sequence of small steps, each one reversible in under a minute, so any step could be undone on its own rather than rolling the whole thing back.
  6. Picked a quiet evening, trained the team first, and ran it: about 90 minutes of work. Customers kept booking throughout.

How it works for the technical reader

The cutover was a big bang by design: all customer-facing surfaces moved at once, because a half-moved booking is worse than either state. What made that safe was everything around it. Every action in the new system sat behind a flag and ran dry until it was proven. A pre-flip gate of about 130 live checks covered each layer end to end, including a real invoice issued and voided, and was re-run until it left no residue. The deployed code was verified to match the repository before the window opened, and the schema's migration ledger was reconciled.

The runbook sequenced the flips so each had its own rollback: freeze, re-sync, funnel, quote and op-form atomically, payments, then the legacy automations off. Old quote and form URLs were bridged to the new routes so in-flight customers were unaffected. A rule that auto-closes stale quotes was deliberately left off until the migrated quotes had been handled. The day after, a leftover automation from the old system was still writing its own price to cards; it was caught on a test booking and every legacy automation was switched off that morning, which is its own case study.
