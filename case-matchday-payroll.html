Systems that run themselves · Events caterer

Matchday payroll eats a whole morning after every game

Now it runs overnight, and only what needs a person gets flagged.

Result
A morning's work, done overnight
My role
Systems & Automation Manager
When
2026 season, 10 games, 162 shifts

The situation

On a football day the business runs around ten food units in a stadium, staffed by a rotating crew. After each game, every worker filled in a form: who they were, which unit, when they started and finished, what break they took.

Then someone spent the whole next morning turning those forms into pay. Matching each form to the posted rota, working out hours and breaks, and catching the mistakes: the same person typed three different ways, a shift sent twice, hours that didn't match what was scheduled.

What went wrong

A shift sent twice gets paid twice. Nobody checking a stack of forms on a tired Monday is going to notice that two of them are the same shift, especially when the name is spelled differently on each.

When the duplicate check went in, it went back over the season so far. It found three shifts that had been submitted twice, and one of them had already been paid. That row was flagged for review instead of quietly staying in the books.

What I did

  1. Found every way the forms went wrong. Names spelled differently, shifts sent twice, breaks written as words, hours that didn't match the rota. Each one became a rule.
  2. Kept it in Google Sheets. The work is seasonal and the team already lived in Sheets, so a spreadsheet with a script behind it was the right size. A custom system would have been more to maintain for the same result.
  3. Gave every person one name. A staff list maps every spelling to one agreed name, so "jon smith" and "J. Smith" pay the same person. Anyone new who picks "Others" on the form triggers an email to me, so the list stays complete.
  4. Caught duplicates, and kept a record. Same person, same date, same unit counts as one shift. The latest submission is kept, and every discarded one is logged with when it was sent.
  5. Checked every shift against the rota. Each payroll line shows whether the person was on the posted rota, their scheduled hours, and how far off they were: on track, over, under, or a major gap.
  6. Made it run on its own. Every night the script cleans the new forms, then builds the payroll lines an hour later. The morning job became reading the flags.

It grew beyond payroll. The same spreadsheet takes each unit's sales report and shows every game's profit: gross sales, VAT, stock, rent, the stadium's admin fee, wages, and wages as a share of sales against target.

How it works for the technical reader

Google Forms feed a response sheet. An Apps Script runs nightly on a time trigger: it builds an alias map from the staff list, resolves every submission to a canonical name, and groups submissions by person, date and unit. The latest in each group is kept; the rest are appended to a hidden audit tab with their row numbers and timestamps. Kept rows are written to a processed tab as formulas, with start and end times rounded to 15 minutes, breaks converted from the form's options to hours, and pay calculated at the rate in force on the shift date, so the April 2026 rate change applied from the right game without touching earlier ones.

An hour later a second trigger creates one payroll line per person per game, skipping any that already exist, so re-runs never double up. Each line looks up the opponent, sums hours and pay, checks the posted rota, and grades the variance against scheduled hours with two thresholds. A system flag marks any line with a duplicate behind it, red if it has already been paid. A form trigger emails me when someone submits as "Others", so the staff list never falls behind.
