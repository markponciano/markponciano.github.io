Numbers you can trust · Inn and rentals

Which business was this spent on?

Every line now belongs to one business, itemised for the accountant to file.

Result
1,230 transactions, six sets of books
My role
Freelance bookkeeper
When
Aug to Sep 2026

The situation

One owner, five businesses, and a year of spending through the same accounts. An inn, three rental properties and a small online business, plus the owner's own spending, all paid from eight bank and card accounts: about $380,000 out and $290,000 in.

The year's books were still to be done when the inn was sold, and its last return had a deadline. The accountant needed each business's expenses, category by category. The bank statements don't say which business a payment was for.

What went wrong

The first version I sent was right, and the owner couldn't use it. I'd built it the way an analyst would: a dashboard, a reconciliation, a tab for every business.

The owner called it "100 pages I have to piece together." What they wanted was the report they already knew from their old software: a list of categories, each with its total and the lines beneath it.

What I did

  1. Put every account in one place. All eight accounts for the full year, each line read once and given to exactly one of six sets of books: the five businesses, or personal. The totals tie back to each bank's own export, so nothing was dropped or counted twice.
  2. Wrote the owner's rules down. The system lived in their head: anything on the main card not marked for another property belongs to the inn; food is never purely personal; online orders split by percentage across the properties. I gathered the rules from emails, a recorded call and handwritten notes into one list, and applied every one.
  3. Named the cheques. 374 of them, identified from photographs. The few I couldn't place went to the owner as a one-page list with a blank "Who was it to?" column.
  4. Rebuilt the report in the owner's shape. One file per property, in the layout they knew. Every category open by default, because their viewer hid the buttons to expand them, and each line written exactly as it appears on the statement, so it could be found.
  5. Left the tax calls with the accountant, and the final say with the owner. 40 possible capital items, the car, and positions the owner wanted to take went to the accountant as a separate note, recorded as the owner's. Then came the owner's review rounds. When the owner suspected a store card had been counted twice, its statement balances proved them right to the cent, and the duplicate came out.

How it works for the technical reader

A Python script reads every bank and card export once into one master workbook and tags each line to a single business, with the owner's rules written into it as code. The reports the owner reads are generated from that master, so the owner's version and the accountant's can never disagree. Each change from a review round is logged against the line it moved. The recorded call was transcribed on my own machine, to capture the rules in the owner's words. The data was kept in a folder outside any synced drive. Files moved through Proton, a private encrypted cloud I suggested so the owner would be comfortable sending financial records, with the password sent separately.
